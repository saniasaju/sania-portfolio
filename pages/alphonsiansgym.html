CASE STUDIES

Portfolio CampInn Alphonsians’ Gym

Overview

Alphonsians’ Gym began as a PGDCA project: a multi-page website for the gym at Alphonsa College, Pala. The original version brought gym information, workout plans, healthy living content, recipes, fitness calculators, membership information, and registration into one place.

A few years later, I returned to the same project with a different skill set and a different goal. I wanted to rebuild the experience around what students actually need, keeping what worked, removing what no longer fit, and making the website more focused on the context.

Challenge

The original website worked as an academic project, but it was built around a broad collection of fitness content rather than the specific context of a college gym.

Some features, particularly membership plans and general registration, did not reflect how Alphonsians’ Gym actually works. Students already have access to the gym. What they need is a clearer way to understand the space, plan their visit, access equipment, and find useful training and wellness support.

The challenge was to turn the existing website into a more focused experience built around those needs.

Solution

I rebuilt the website around the student’s gym journey.

  • Membership plans became slot booking
  • Gym information became a dedicated The Gym experience
  • Workout guidance moved into Train, nutrition
  • Recipes into Wellness
  • The fitness calculators remained available as tools

The result is a website with a clearer purpose: helping students understand the gym, prepare for a visit, book a suitable slot, and access useful resources around their training.

Existing Experience

I began by looking at what still made sense, what needed to change, and how the experience could better reflect the way students would actually use the gym.

Some ideas from the previous version of the website were still useful.

  • The fitness calculators, workout guidance and wellness information could support students outside the gym
  • information about the gym itself was still important

Other parts felt disconnected from the actual context.

The biggest example was membership. Alphonsians’ Gym is part of a women’s college and is available to students, so presenting commercial-style membership plans did not make much sense. The problem was accessing a shared gym at a suitable time.

That observation became the starting point for the new booking experience.

User Journey

Instead of deciding on pages first, I thought about the experience in stages:

Understand the gym, available equipment, and facilities.

Access workout guidance, fitness calculators, recipes, and wellness resources to support training.

Choose a suitable date and time, select the required equipment, and check availability.

Thinking about the experience this way helped me separate needs that had previously been grouped together and gave each part of the website a clearer purpose.

Information Architecture

The older structure included pages such as Prices and Plans, Healthy Living, Personal Workout Plans, Contact Us, and About Us.

For the rebuild, I reorganised the information architecture around what students might need at different points in their gym experience:

Home introduces the gym and directs students towards the most important actions.

Booking focuses entirely on planning a gym visit through date, time, and equipment selection.

Calculators brings BMI and calorie calculations into a dedicated utility space.

Train contains structured workout guidance for different fitness goals.

Wellness brings together nutrition, recipes, and healthy-living content.

Login explores how a future student account experience could work.

About helps students understand the physical space, available equipment, facilities, access, and people behind it.

Each page now has a clearer reason to exist. Once the structure was established, I moved into individual flows and page layouts.

Booking Flow

Booking was the most significant new feature, so I treated it as the central interaction of the redesign. I broke the task into a simple sequence:

01 — Choose a date and time

A student first chooses when she wants to visit

02 — Select equipment

Then selects the equipment she plans to use

03 — Check availability

The system checks the preferred combination, if not available, it offers alternatives based on availability

04 — Confirm

Then the available slot booking is confirmed

Working through that logic helped me think beyond the ideal path and consider how the system should respond to different user choices.

Visual System

Once the overall direction was clear, design and development became a more continuous process.

After establishing the structure and main interactions, I developed a visual system to accompany it.

The original project relied more heavily on common Bootstrap patterns and page-by-page styling. For the rebuild, I moved towards a more editorial visual style while retaining the brand colours of Alphonsa College.

Development

I built the redesigned website using HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, with reusable components for shared elements such as the navigation and footer.

The website was initially developed for desktop and then adapted for tablet and mobile layouts.

This involved the restructuring of layouts, content required different spacing, and interactions had to remain usable across smaller viewport sizes.

The project also involved a significant amount of iterative debugging. Navigation behaviour, reusable components, media sizing, animations, responsive layouts, stacking, and JavaScript interactions all required repeated testing and adjustment before they behaved as intended.

Rather than treating development as the final step after design, implementation became another way of testing whether the design actually worked.

Vibe Coding

This was also one of the projects where AI-assisted development became an intentional part of my workflow.

I used AI to explore implementation approaches, troubleshoot problems, and speed up repetitive coding tasks. But generated code was rarely the end of the process.

I still needed to describe the intended behaviour precisely, which helped me improve my prompting skills, understand how different pieces of code worked together, test the result, identify conflicts, and refine the implementation.

AI became part of the development process, but the decisions, testing, and refinement still depended on understanding what the experience needed to do.

Accessibility

Accessibility was considered throughout development, including semantic HTML, keyboard interaction, visible focus states, clear controls, responsive layouts, and reduced-motion behaviour where appropriate.

These considerations were treated as part of the interface rather than something added after the main experience was complete.

Final Experience

The final Alphonsians’ Gym website is a responsive front-end experience designed around the different stages of a student’s connection with the gym.

Compared with the original project, the biggest improvement is that the website now has a more specific understanding of who it is for and what they need from it.

The project currently remains an interactive front-end prototype. There is no live database, authentication system, or backend booking infrastructure, so interactions such as booking and login demonstrate the intended product behaviour rather than functioning as an institutional service.

Reflection

Returning to an older project with the design and development skills I had gained since building the original version led me to approach it very differently.

  • The original website was a group project and, because it was academic, focused more heavily on development than design. Rebuilding it independently made me think more deeply about why a page or feature should exist in the first place.
  • Prioritising the context, identifying the most useful tasks, and organising the experience around them gave the redesign a much clearer direction.
  • The development process also influenced my design decisions. Responsive behaviour forced me to question layouts that worked only at one screen size, while reusable components encouraged greater consistency across the website.
  • AI helped me move through development faster, but it also reinforced something important:
  • AI made development faster. It didn’t remove the need to think.

I still had to define what I wanted, understand whether a solution made sense, test it in context, debug failures, and recognise when the design itself needed to change.

For me, the biggest learning from this project was how to think through, design, and build a digital experience as one connected process.
